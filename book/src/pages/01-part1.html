Chapter 1

Welcome to Edusim

Edusim is a 3D world that runs in your web browser. You walk around it, you build things in it, and then you write programs that make those things move, talk and light up.

It's free, there's nothing to download and nobody needs an account. If a computer can open a web page, it can run Edusim – school Chromebooks included.

Three things you'll do
Build itStretch, stack and join four simple shapes into anything you can picture: a train, a turtle, a whole town.
repeat 4
Code itSnap coding blocks together to make what you built move, turn, glide and talk.
Explore itVisit worlds made for learning: dinosaurs, the Moon, a coral reef, the inside of your body.

A T. rex, a coral reef, and a town you'll build and program yourself.

Everything in Edusim is measured in feet, and you are 5 feet tall. That makes you the ruler: a building “twice your height” is 10 feet tall.
The Park from the air. This is the first world you'll see – and every tree, path and building in it is something you can walk up to.
A world, not a game

There's no score in Edusim and no way to lose. Instead there are worlds to explore, things to read, and empty space waiting for whatever you decide to build.

The worlds were made by the same people who made Edusim. Each one is full of signs that teach something, and challenge boards that ask you to program the things standing in it.

Two Canada geese on the Park's pond.
Open Edusim
  1. Open your web browser – Chrome is best – and click in the address bar at the top.
  2. Type edusim3dweb.com/app/ and press Enter.
  3. Wait a few seconds while the world builds itself. The first time, you'll arrive at the gate of a city park.
  4. Look around. Hold the mouse button down and drag, or drag with a finger on a tablet. The view turns with you.
Where you arrive: the gate of Franklin Park. The panel on the left is a real web page, standing in the world. If you only see a blank page, check the address has /app/ on the end, then press refresh. If the world is still empty after a minute, try Chrome. Edusim runs as an ordinary web page, so there's nothing for a technology department to install. Students' work stays in their own browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

Why 3D?

When you turn a shape in your head, picture how parts fit together, or find your way around a new place, you're using spatial reasoning. It is one of the most useful kinds of thinking there is – and you can get better at it.

Scientists who study spatial reasoning usually split it into four skills. Each one has a badge, and you'll see these badges beside the projects in this book.

Turning a thing in your head. Picturing what an object looks like from another side, without walking round it.

Picturing a change. Imagining a shape being stretched, folded or built up from parts before you do it.

Knowing where you are. Finding your way, reading a map, knowing which way you're facing and which way is back.

How parts fit together. Seeing which piece goes where, what is touching what, and what is on top of what.

What the scientists found

Researchers have tested spatial training in hundreds of studies. When lots of studies are added together it's called a meta-analysis, and it tells you how big a difference something really makes.

Why it matters

One study followed hundreds of thousands of students for decades. Teenagers with strong spatial skills were more likely to end up as scientists, engineers and designers – even compared with students who had the same maths and reading scores.

Spatial reasoning is trainable, it helps with maths and science, and it is hardly ever taught on purpose. Building and coding in 3D is one way to practise it on purpose.

Building a train from shapes trains three of the four skills at once.
How Edusim uses the research
  • You build from shapes, because putting a model together from parts is exactly the kind of practice the studies measured.
  • Pieces turn in steps of 15 degrees, so you have to picture the turn before you make it, and square corners come out square.
  • Worlds are places you walk through, which trains your sense of where you are far better than looking at a flat picture.
  • Programs move things in 3D. To make a train drive a square you have to imagine the turns from the train's point of view.
These studies tested 3D learning and spatial training in general. Nobody has yet run a big study of Edusim itself. What we can say is that Edusim was designed around what the research found works. The turning rings snap every 15 degrees, so you plan a turn before you make it.
