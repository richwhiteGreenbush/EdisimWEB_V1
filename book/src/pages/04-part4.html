Your first program

A program is a list of instructions. A computer follows them one at a time, in order, exactly as written – even when they're wrong.

Your turtle's first program: one block that says Hello! In Edusim you write programs with blocks: you drag them into a list instead of typing. It's the same idea professional coders use; they just type the words.
Open the Program editor
  1. Click your turtle (or any model you've rendered).
  2. Click Program. The editor opens.

The blocks in this book are printed in Edusim's own colours, so the orange block on the page is the orange block on your screen. All 20 are on pages –.
Program 1: Say hello
  1. Find the purple say [Hello!] block in the palette. It's under Look, so scroll down.
  2. Drag it into the big white space on the right and let go.
  3. Click the white box inside the block and type Hello!
  4. Click Save.
say [Hello!]

A speech bubble pops up over your turtle for a few seconds. You've written a program!

Open Program again, change the words, and Save. What's the longest thing your turtle will say?
Program 2: A list of steps

Add four more blocks under say. Each block snaps in below the one before it.

say [Hello!] wait [1] seconds move forward [5] feet rotate [180] degrees go back to start
1Says hello
2Waits one second
3Walks 5 feet forward
4Turns round
5Jumps back to where it began
The same program in the editor. Swap the order: put rotate [180] degrees before move forward [5] feet. Now which way does it walk? Order matters.

The green play button

Once a model has a program, a green play button floats above it. Click it to run the program again from the top, as many times as you like.

A model's program is saved with your world. Come back tomorrow and it will start running all by itself. Has your model wandered off? Put go back to start at the end of your program. It puts the model back where it was, facing the way it faced, at the size it was, when the program began.
Program 3: Do it again with repeat

repeat [10] times is shaped like a mouth. Whatever you put inside it happens again and again. This turtle jumps, then looks round in four quarter turns:

move up by [2] feet wait [0.3] seconds move up by [-2] feet repeat [4] times { rotate [90] degrees wait [0.5] seconds } Drop blocks inside the orange jaw of repeat. A minus sign turns a block round: move up by [-2] feet moves down. What does rotate [-90] degrees do?
Program 4: Forever

forever is a repeat that never stops. This one makes your turtle wander about the field all day:

forever { glide [3] feet over [2] seconds [smoothly] wait [1] seconds rotate [30] degrees } Glide moves a model slowly instead of jumping. You'll use it a lot in Chapter 13. A forever program really does run forever, even after you close the tab and come back. To stop it, open Program, click Clear, then Save.
Program 5: Now you see it

set opacity to [50] % makes a model see-through. At 100 you can't see through it at all; at 0 it's invisible.

repeat [3] times { set opacity to [30] % wait [1] seconds set opacity to [100] % wait [1] seconds } The turtle at 30%: half-hidden in the grass. Use forever, change size by [10] %, set size to [100] % and two wait [1] seconds blocks to make your turtle swell up and shrink back, over and over. Hint: set size to 100% puts it back to exactly the size it started.

Chapter 13

Make the train go

Your engine glides along a track, turns corners, draws shapes on the grass, and runs to a timetable.

The engine halfway through a smooth 20-foot glide. The engine you built in Chapter 9, in an open field with room to move. No engine? Every program in this chapter works on any model.
Program 6: Glide

move forward [2] feet jumps there in an instant. A train shouldn't jump: it should roll. That's what glide is for.

glide [20] feet over [4] seconds [smoothly]
smoothlySpeeds up gently and slows down at the end, like a real train.
straightThe same speed all the way, then a sudden stop.
bouncyOvershoots a little and bounces back. Funny on an animal!
Keep the 20 feet and change the seconds. 2 seconds is an express train; 10 is a slow goods train.
Which way is a turn?

Moving always goes forward: the way the model's front is pointing. So to change where it goes, turn it first. Here's what rotate [90] degrees does, looking down from the sky:

rotate [90] degreesA quarter turn to the left
rotate [-90] degreesA quarter turn to the right
rotate [180] degreesTurn right round
rotate [360] degreesAll the way round, back where it started

Grey: the way it was facing. Black: the way it faces after the turn.

Stand up and do it yourself. Face the front of the room and turn 90 degrees to the left. Now 180. Which wall are you facing?
Program 7: Draw a square

The marker blocks give your model a pen. While the marker is down, it leaves a coloured line on the ground wherever it goes.

marker color [#e0455f] marker down repeat [4] times { glide [20] feet over [4] seconds [smoothly] rotate [90] degrees } Click the colour box in marker color to pick the pen's colour. The marker draws a real 3D tube, three inches thick. You can walk round your drawing and look at it from any side.
Four sides, four left turns: the engine ends up exactly where it started.
Why it closes

Every time the engine reaches a corner it turns 90 degrees. Four corners make 4 × 90 = 360 degrees: one whole turn. So by the end, it's facing the way it started – and back where it started.

If the square comes out lopsided, check you put the rotate inside the repeat. Outside it, the engine turns once at the very end instead of at every corner.

To wipe the drawing away, use erase all marks. It wipes every mark in the world, not just this model's.

The 360 rule

Any shape with straight, equal sides works the same way. Divide 360 by the number of sides, and that's the turn at each corner.

ShapeSidesRepeatTurn
Triangle33 times360 ÷ 3 = 120
Square44 times360 ÷ 4 = 90
Pentagon55 times360 ÷ 5 = 72
Hexagon66 times360 ÷ 6 = 60
Octagon88 times360 ÷ 8 = 45
marker color [#8a5cf5] marker down repeat [6] times { glide [12] feet over [1] seconds [smoothly] rotate [60] degrees } marker up
How big is the turn for a shape with 10 sides? With 12? With 36? Try the last one: what does it look like? (Answers: 36, 30 and 10 degrees – and 36 sides is nearly a circle.) This is the exterior-angle theorem, discovered rather than taught: the turns of any closed walk around a convex polygon add up to 360°.
Program 8: The timetable

Real trains run back and forth between stations all day. This program does the same, forever:

forever { glide [30] feet over [5] seconds [smoothly] say [All aboard!] wait [3] seconds rotate [180] degrees } Build a second station 30 feet in front of your engine. Does the train stop right beside it? If it overshoots, change the 30.
A five-pointed star is five straight lines with a sharp turn of 144 degrees at each point. Write the program, using the square on page as your guide.

Hint: 144 is a big turn, more than a right angle. Five turns of 144 add up to 720 – two whole turns – which is why the lines cross over each other.

Chapter 14

Bring the animal to life

Models can listen. When one says something, another can hear it and answer – and that's how you make a world where things happen together.

The engine calls "All aboard!" and the turtle answers. Anything with a program can talk and listen: an engine, an animal, a house, even a light orb.
Program 9: Listen and answer

when an object says [hello] waits quietly until another model says those words. Then it runs whatever is inside it.

  1. Program the turtle with this, and Save. Nothing happens yet: it's listening.
when an object says [All aboard!] { say [Wait for me!] }
  1. Program the engine with just this, and Save.
say [All aboard!] The turtle's program. The listening block wraps round the answer. The words have to match. Capital letters don't matter, but spelling and the ! do: All aboard won't hear All aboard!
Program 10: A longer answer

A listening block can hold a whole program. When the turtle hears the engine, it answers, bounces forward, turns round and says goodbye:

when an object says [All aboard!] { say [Wait for me!] glide [10] feet over [3] seconds [bouncy] rotate [180] degrees say [Bye!] } The timetable program on page already says All aboard! at every stop. Give it to the engine, and the turtle answers every time the train pulls in.
Program 11: A chain reaction

A model that hears something can say something too, and another model can be listening for that. Three models make a chain:

Enginesay [All aboard!]
→
Turtlewhen an object says [All aboard!] { say [Wait for me!] }
→
Giraffewhen an object says [Wait for me!] { move up by [1] feet wait [0.3] seconds move up by [-1] feet }
Listening blocks never start themselves again while they're still running, so two models answering each other can't get stuck shouting forever. This is event-driven programming, the way apps and games respond to clicks and messages. Ask students to draw the chain as boxes and arrows before they build it.
Build two more animals (Chapter 10 has ideas) and give each one an act. When you say "Showtime!", every animal should start its act.
  1. Program every animal with its act, and Save. They wait, listening.
  2. Make a light orb and give it the program below. When you Save it, the show starts.
  3. Click the orb's green play button to run the show again.

How can you say something? Give a light orb a one-block program: say [Showtime!]. Clicking its green play button is your starting whistle.

The whole town: every model in it was built in this book.
engine and carriage (Ch. 9) turtle (Ch. 10) station (Ch. 11) house (Ch. 11)
Program 12: Build a street with code

Building one house takes a while. Building three more takes one block. duplicate [4] ft away makes a copy of the model, a set distance along.

repeat [3] times { duplicate [15] ft away } One house, three copies, 15 feet apart. Copies get their own copy of the program – except the duplicate block. If they kept it, every copy would make copies of its own, and your world would fill up in seconds. Copies are real models. Click one and give it a different colour or a program of its own.
Program 13: Street lamps

when [the sun goes down] listens to the sky instead of to another model.

  1. Make a light orb beside the station: Create Model ▸ Load Object ▸ Light Orb.
  2. Program it with this, and Save.
when [the sun goes down] { change color to [#f2c94c] }
  1. Open Look Around and drag the Time of day slider towards evening. As the sun sets, the lamp turns warm yellow.
The same block can listen for the sun comes up, it is the middle of the day, it gets dark and this world opens. Click its white box to choose.
