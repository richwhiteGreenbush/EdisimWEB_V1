🎒 Student lesson guide

Getting there: press Open this world in Edusim on this page. No account or download. Type it in: edusim3dweb.com/worlds/world.php?slug=atom-hall Opening a world replaces what is in your copy. Use ☰ Menu → Save World first if you have something to keep.

How it works: walk with the arrow keys and drag to look around. Every tower has its numbers on a shopfront at street level. To run a program, click the green ▶ above an object. To read or change one, click the object and press Program.

How to read the city: height is measured from the ground. When a tower shows atomic radius, 1 foot is 5 picometres. When it shows electronegativity, 15 feet is 1 unit. A dark stump means there is no value, not a value of zero.

4 · The salt crystal

South of the city. One half is packed, with the ions at their true sizes. Walk into the other half, where they are shrunk so you can see the pattern. Purple is sodium, green is chloride.

  1. Stand next to one sodium ion in the open half. How many chloride neighbours touch its guide lines? And how many sodiums round a chloride?
  2. Find a pair that "belongs together" as one salt molecule. Can you? What does that tell you?
  3. From the placard: Na+ is pm and Cl- is pm. Which ion is bigger, by what factor? Sodium is the larger atom. Why might its ion be the smaller one?

🍎 Teacher lesson card

ForGrades 8–10 physical science or first-year chemistry, and computer science. No coding experience needed: students begin by pressing a button and changing one number in a green block. One activity has them build a short program from empty.
TimeOne 45-minute period for the skyline and the tank, a 90-minute block for everything, or one section a day through a unit on the periodic table, bonding or states of matter.
You needA browser on each computer (Chromebooks are fine). No account, install or student data. Printed copies of the student guide or a way to type answers.
Students willRead periodic trends in atomic radius and electronegativity, and explain them from shells and nuclear charge. Relate lone pairs to bond angles. Describe an ionic lattice and why it has no molecules. Use a model to connect temperature, particle speed and state, and say where that model falls short.
Say this out loud. Tower height is a chart of a number, not a picture of an atom: a cesium tower is not 48.8 feet of anything. Radii here are covalent radii (Cordero and others, 2008), so they will differ from a textbook that prints empirical or van der Waals values, and carbon is its single-bond value. The tank is a rule-based model: a particle melts because an if block says so, not because forces let go. It leaves out forces between particles, leaves out particle mass, and has no pause at a melting point. Speed is in feet per step, so compare ratios and do not convert it to metres per second. Research supports 3D learning environments in general, but nobody has tested this particular world.

Discussion prompts

  • Why does the same pair of ideas, shells and nuclear charge, explain both trends?
  • Two towers can differ by 40 feet and the atoms by a few picometres. What does a chart like this make easy to see, and what can it hide?
  • A dark stump and a zero are different claims. Why does the difference matter in science?
  • The tank's particles change state at an exact number. What would you have to add to make that happen on its own?

Answer key

Show the answers
1 RadiusDown column 1: Li 128, Na 166, K 203, Rb 220, Cs 244 pm, which is 25.6, 33.2, 40.6, 44.0 and 48.8 ft. Across period 2: Li 128, Be 96, B 84, C 76, N 71, O 66, F 57 pm. Biggest at the bottom left: francium, 260 pm and 52.0 ft. Down a column each period adds a shell further out. Across a period protons are added while the shell stays the same, so the electrons are pulled in harder. Zr and Hf are both 175 pm despite Hf's extra shell: the 14 lanthanides between them add protons that their f electrons shield poorly, which is the lanthanide contraction.
2 ElectronegativityCs 0.79, Na 0.93, H 2.20, C 2.55, Cl 3.16, O 3.44, F 3.98. The top right becomes tallest, with fluorine at 59.7 ft, and cesium falls to 11.9 ft. A small atom's bonding electrons sit closer to the nucleus and feel more of its charge. He, Ne, Ar and Rn have no value on this scale, since it is worked out from bonds and they form few or none. Differences: C–H 0.35 (barely polar), O–H 1.24 (polar covalent), Na–Cl 2.23 (ionic).
3 MoleculesMethane CH4: 4 bonded, 0 lone pairs, 109.5°, tetrahedral. Ammonia NH3: 3 bonded, 1 lone pair, 106.7° (the placard rounds to 107°), pyramid. Water H2O: 2 bonded, 2 lone pairs, 104.5°, bent. Carbon dioxide CO2: 2 double bonds, 0 lone pairs on carbon, 180°, straight. Lone pairs take up more room than bonds and squeeze the angle. Benzene's six ring bonds are all the same length, 0.139 nm, between single and double, because those electrons are shared round the ring.
4 SaltSix neighbours each way. No pair belongs together: there is no NaCl molecule, only a repeating lattice held by every neighbour's charge. Na+ 102 pm, Cl- 181 pm, so chloride is about 1.77 times bigger. Sodium's atom is larger than chlorine's, but it loses its outer shell to become an ion, and chlorine gains an electron with no new protons. The model holds 216 ions; a 1 mm grain has about 4.5 x 10^19.
5 Tank100 K solid, speed 0.20. 250 K solid, 0.32. 300 K liquid, 0.35. 400 K gas, 0.40. 900 K gas, 0.60. Each is the shipped water settings: melts 273, boils 373, and the model changes state exactly at those numbers. 100 to 400 K is 4 times hotter and 2 times faster; 100 to 900 K is 9 times hotter and 3 times faster: speed goes as the square root of absolute temperature. The rule is "set root to square root of t" followed by "set speed to root × 0.02". A particle reads speed and state and never reads temperature. The particle itself does not change, only its motion. At 0 K speed is 0 and the crystal stands still (reachable in this model, not in reality). At −50 the thermostat says nothing can be colder than 0 K and uses 0, since kelvins start at absolute zero.
6 SubstancesAt 300 K: nitrogen gas; ethanol, water and mercury liquid; table salt and iron solid. Salt holds on because every ion is pulled by all its oppositely charged neighbours, while water molecules attract each other far more weakly. A real pot stays at 273 K because the heat goes into breaking particles out of the crystal rather than speeding them up, which is the latent heat of melting. The model has no energy term, so it cannot show this.

Adjusting it

  • Middle school: keep Sections 1, 3 (methane and water only) and 5. Skip the difference calculation and the odd pair.
  • Chemistry: add first ionization energy as a prediction students test against a data table, and name VSEPR once they have seen the angles.
  • Physics: real speed goes as the square root of T over mass. Have students say what they would change on the thermometer, then why two substances at one temperature would differ.
  • Computer science: trace how a number travels from the thermostat through the thermometer to a particle, and discuss why one object owns it.
  • Handing work in: ☰ Menu → Save World downloads a file you can open and inspect, programs included.

Quick troubleshooting

  • Clicking a tower does nothing: that is deliberate. The whole city is one object. Read the shopfront, and use the buttons to change it.
  • Pressing a button changes nothing: click the green ▶ floating above it, not the button itself.
  • A message says nothing has set a value: someone removed a share block, or deleted the thermostat. Reopen the world for a fresh copy.
  • The tank will not follow the thermostat after the heater ran: the burner shared a newer temperature. Press the thermostat's green ▶ to take it back.
  • The thermometer column stops at the top: its scale ends at 600 K. The tank and the speed reading still work above that.
  • The tank runs slowly: speed is per step, so a slower computer shows the same model in slow motion. The numbers do not change.
