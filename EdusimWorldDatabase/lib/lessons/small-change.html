🍎 Teacher guide

ForGrades 9–12, personal finance, math or computer science. No coding experience needed: students mostly change one number.
TimeOne 45-minute period for three stations, a 90-minute block for all six, or one station a day as openers.
You needA browser on each computer (Chromebooks are fine). No account, install or student data. Print the student card, or have students type answers.
Students willCalculate take-home pay. Build a budget. Explain compounding, inflation and diversification from a working model. Compare the true cost of credit. Weigh insurance against savings. Spot a scam.
Say this out loud. Every rate in this world is a classroom example, not a prediction or financial advice. The investing chart uses invented returns and treats companies as independent, so it overstates how much diversifying helps, though the direction is right. Research supports financial education and 3D learning in general, but nobody has tested this particular world.

Discussion prompts

  • On the Station 3 board, the later starter paid in $12,000 less and ended with about $203,000 less. Where did the rest of that gap come from?
  • Why might a lender be happy with a $15 minimum payment on a $500 balance?
  • The lower car payment costs more in total. When could it still be the right choice?
  • Who can afford to start at 18? What should a good plan look like for someone who can't?

Adjusting it

  • New to coding: only change numbers at the top of each program, and use the two block activities.
  • Algebra II: derive the savings total as a geometric series, then check it against the Station 3 board.
  • Computer science: add an employer match to a sprout's program, or a second fee to the market model.
  • Handing work in: ☰ Menu → Save World downloads a file you can open and inspect, programs included.

Teach it with care

Money is personal, and students' families are in very different places. The examples use a fictional first job on purpose, so nobody has to share their own numbers. Avoid asking about family income or debt, and don't suggest that being short of money comes from bad choices: wages, costs and luck matter too. The models show how the math works, not who deserves what.

Quick troubleshooting

  • The menu opens the wrong thing: click the coins in the jar, not the glass; the balloon's body, not its string.
  • A program stops with a message: a typo. Fix it in the Program panel and press Save; nothing else breaks.
  • The world has been changed: reopen it from the gallery for a fresh copy.
  • The chart is crowded: reopen the world, since each ▶ adds a line and marks are not saved.
