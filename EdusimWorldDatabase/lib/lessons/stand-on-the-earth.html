🎒 Student lesson guide

Getting there: press Open this world in Edusim on this page. No account or download. Type it in: edusim3dweb.com/worlds/world.php?slug=stand-on-the-earth Opening a world replaces what is in your copy. Use ☰ Menu → Save World first if you have something to keep.

How it works: walk with the arrow keys and drag to look around. To read or change a program, click an object and press Program. Change a number in a green block and press Save. A green ▶ runs a program again.

Where you are: you are standing on the Earth's North Pole, looking out at the Moon and the Sun. Sizes and distances here are not to scale, so that everything fits. Section 1 and the board on the floor tell you what the real ones are.

Name Date

1 · Half lit, always

Do not move yet. Look at the ground, then turn slowly all the way round.

  1. Before you look: why do you think the Moon changes shape?
  2. How much of the Earth under your feet is lit? Where is the edge between day and night?
  3. Walk down off the globe and look back up at a thin crescent moon from beside it. Is it still only a thin crescent? How much of it is really lit?
  4. So what is changing during a month: how much of the Moon is lit, or how much of the lit part we can see?

3 · Program the Moon

Find the MOON CLOCK by your feet. Click it → Program. Its green numbers run the Moon.

  1. ACTIVITY 1: set dayStep to 0.2 and press Save, then try 0.01. The clock names each phase as it arrives. Say the next one before it does. Which phase comes after full moon?
  2. How many days does the clock count from one new moon to the next? Which block says so?
  3. Look at the Moon's markings as it goes round. Do they ever turn away from you?
  4. ACTIVITY 2: click the moving Moon → Program, and drag out its rotate block. Press Save. Predict first. What did it do? Put the block back and press Save.
  5. That one block turns the Moon as it goes. Why does it mean we only ever see one side?

🍎 Teacher lesson card

ForGrades 5–8 Earth and space science, and computer science. No coding experience needed: students begin by turning on the spot and changing one number in a green block. One activity has them build a short program from empty, and the sine and arccosine in the column are there to read, not to be taught.
TimeOne 45-minute period for either the Moon or the seasons, a 90-minute block for both, or two lessons a week apart.
You needA browser on each computer. No account, install or student data. Printed copies of the student guide or a way to type answers.
Students willExplain that half the Moon is always lit and that phases come from how much of the lit half we see. Name the phases in order and connect each to the time it is highest. Explain that seasons come from the Earth's fixed tilt and not from its distance from the Sun. Use a block model to predict the noon Sun and the hours of daylight at any latitude.
Say this out loud. Nothing here is to scale, and it could not be: on a globe this size the true Moon would be over a mile away. The true-scale Earth and Moon on the floor show that. The Sun in the Moon lab does not move, so one trip round takes 29.5 days, the true time from new moon to new moon; the real Moon goes round in 27.3 days and needs about two more to catch up, because the Earth has moved on. The lit part of each moon is worked out from the Sun's direction and not painted, but this world does not model eclipses, the Moon's real tilted orbit, or the bending of light by the air, so real day lengths run a few minutes longer than the column says. It also does not model temperature: the two reasons for summer are for students to reason out. Part of MS-ESS1-1 is eclipses, so teach that part another way. Research supports 3D learning environments in general, but nobody has tested this particular world.

Misconceptions this world is built to surface

  • "The dark part of the Moon is the Earth's shadow." Walking round a crescent shows the far half lit, and at full moon the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon and there is no shadow on it.
  • "Summer is when we are closer to the Sun." The Earth is closest in early January, and the two hemispheres have opposite seasons.
  • "The Earth's axis wobbles through the year toward and away from the Sun." The red tip points the same way all year. What changes is where the Earth is.
  • "The far side of the Moon is always dark." It gets as much sun as the near side. It is only always turned away from us.

Discussion prompts

  • If you stood on the Moon during a full moon, what would the Earth look like?
  • Why don't we get an eclipse every month, if the Moon passes the Sun's side every new moon?
  • If the Earth had no tilt, what would a year be like in Kansas? At the North Pole?
  • The hottest weeks in Kansas come after the longest day. What might explain the delay?

Answer key

Show the answers
1 Half litHalf the Earth is lit; the edge runs under the pole here. From beside it, a crescent moon is half lit: half of the Moon is lit at all times. Phases are about how much of the lit half can be seen from Earth, not how much is lit.
2 PhasesTime printed under each: new moon NOON, waxing crescent 3 PM, first quarter 6 PM, waxing gibbous 9 PM, full moon MIDNIGHT, waning gibbous 3 AM, last quarter 6 AM, waning crescent 9 AM. Waxing moons are lit on the right and waning moons on the left, seen from the north. A full moon is highest at midnight and rises around sunset. A half moon high at sunset is first quarter, because it stands over 6 PM. At new moon the Sun is on the same side of Earth as the Moon, and at full moon on the opposite side. The dark part is the Moon's own night; at full moon the Earth is between them yet the Moon is fully lit.
3 ProgramAfter full moon comes waning gibbous. The clock counts 29.5 days, set by the orbitDays block. The markings stay toward you. Without rotate the Moon carries on in a straight line and leaves the world, since turning is what bends its path. The same block turns the Moon once per trip round, so the same face stays toward the middle.
4 SeasonsJune: the tip leans toward the lamp, and most of the ring is lit. December: the tip leans away, and less of the ring is lit. March and September: the tip points sideways, and about half is lit. The tip never changes direction; what changes is where the Earth is in its orbit. The Earth is closest in early January, at 91.4 million miles, and farthest in early July, at 94.5 million miles. Evidence: it is closest in northern winter, and the hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same distance.
5 KansasThe column says: March 53 degrees and 12 hours, June 76 degrees and 14.6 hours, September 52 degrees and 12 hours, December 29 degrees and 9.4 hours. A high Sun puts the same light on a smaller patch of ground. June is warmer because the Sun is higher and the day is longer.
6 ElsewhereEquator: 12 hours in both months. Sydney: 9.7 hours in June and 14.3 in December, so opposite to Kansas at the same distance from the Sun. Latitude 70, once guarded: 24 hours in June and 0 in December, the midnight sun and polar night. The longest day and the highest Sun arrive together.
Exit ticketAt the true scale, 60 feet. A Moon that small and that far away is hard to see at all.

Adjusting it

  • Upper elementary: keep Sections 1, 2 and 4, and leave the Program panel for a demonstration.
  • Earth science: add the Moon's 5° orbital tilt to explain why eclipses are rare, using the discussion prompt and a lamp and ball.
  • Math: read the column's sine as a model of a yearly cycle, and graph the noon Sun across the Calendar's year.
  • Computer science: trace how yearDay travels from the dial through the column to the Earth, and discuss why a value with no answer needs guarding.
  • Handing work in: ☰ Menu → Save World downloads a file you can open and inspect, programs included.

Quick troubleshooting

  • Clicking the Earth, the lamp or a fixed moon does nothing: that is deliberate. The moving Moon, the Moon Clock, the Season Dial, the gold column and the Calendar are the ones with programs.
  • The Moon has flown away: a student removed its rotate block. Put it back and press Save, or reopen the world.
  • A message says a value has no answer: that is Section 6, at a latitude past about 66.5.
  • The season Earth does not follow the dial after the Calendar ran: the Calendar shared a newer day. Press the dial's green ▶ to take it back.
  • The world goes dark or bright with the Time of Day slider: the moons and globes do not change, on purpose. Their light comes from the Sun in the lesson, not from the slider.
  • Things look slow: a slower computer shows the same model in slow motion. The numbers do not change.
