Getting there: press Open this world in Edusim on this page. No account or download. Type it in: edusim3dweb.com/worlds/world.php?slug=the-hall-of-light Opening a world replaces what is in your copy. Use ☰ Menu → Save World first if you have something to keep.

How it works: walk with the arrow keys and drag to look around. Every beam of light is a small glowing bead running a program, drawing its path as it goes. To read or change a program, click the bead (or the lens) and press Program. Change a number in a green block and press Save. A green ▶ runs it again. The red CLEAR button in the middle of the hall wipes every beam.

Angles: on these benches an angle like -25 is measured from the flat, and a minus sign means the beam points down. At a mirror or a surface, the angles i and r are measured from the normal, the dashed line standing straight out of the surface.

🍎 Teacher lesson card

Grades7–12. Sections 1, 2 and 3 suit middle school; Sections 4, 5 and 6 use sine and arcsine.
TimeOne 45-minute period for three benches, or a 90-minute block for all six.
You needA browser on each computer. No account, install or student data. Printed copies of the student guide or a way to type answers.
Students willUse ray models to predict reflection and refraction, apply the law of reflection and Snell's law, explain total internal reflection and the critical angle, explain dispersion as each colour having its own refractive index, and find the focus of a converging lens. Read and change block programs that carry these rules.
Say this out loud. These are ray diagrams, not light. Real light is a wave, and this world ignores that: there is no diffraction, no interference, no partial reflection at glass, and no light lost on the way. Each beam is a program that keeps its own position and is told where every surface is in its green blocks; it cannot detect glass it was not told about. The light is slowed down about a hundred million times: real light crosses this 30-foot hall in about 30 nanoseconds. The prism is dense flint glass (Schott SF11), because in ordinary window glass the colours separate by only 1.4 degrees, narrower than a beam here. The lens uses the thin-lens rule, which is exact for this model and approximate for a real lens. The benches are larger than real lab equipment; angles do not depend on size. Research supports 3D learning environments in general, but nobody has tested this particular world.

Misconceptions this world is built to surface

  • "Light bends because the glass pulls it." It bends because it changes speed crossing the surface. The water placard and the beam's own n say so; a beam that meets the surface straight on, along the normal, does not bend at all.
  • "Angles are measured from the surface." For a flat mirror either way gives equal angles, but Snell's law only works with angles from the normal, so every bench measures from the normal.
  • "Glass makes the colours." The white beam already holds them; the prism only bends each by a different amount.
  • "Total internal reflection is a special kind of mirror." It is Snell's law having no answer: no angle has a sine above 1.

Discussion prompts

  • Why does a straw in a glass of water look broken at the surface?
  • A diamond sparkles more than glass cut the same way. Its critical angle is only 24.4°. How might that help?
  • Fibre optic cables carry most of the world's internet traffic as light. Why does the light not leak out at the bends?
  • Every beam here was told where the glass was. How would you write a program that found out for itself?

3 Water50° in air, 35.08° in water: toward the normal. r is 50° with n = 1 (no bend), 35.08° for water, 30.94° for acrylic, 18.48° for diamond. The bigger n, the more it bends toward the normal. Aim nearer: the grey line of sight lands beyond the real fish, so the fish is closer and deeper than it looks.
4 Light pipet is 0.76 at 30°, 0.98 at 40°, 1.07 at 45° and 1.32 at 60°. At 30 and 40 the beam leaves the glass, bending away from the normal (to 49.46° and 77.70°). At 45 and 60, before the fix, asin has no answer and the beam goes wrong; after the fix it reflects back into the glass. No angle has a sine above 1. The critical angle is 41.1° for this glass (48.6° for water, 42.2° for acrylic, 24.4° for diamond). The rod makes 4 bounces at 18°, 7 bounces at 30, and at 50 the wall angle is only 40°, under acrylic's 42.2°, so the light escapes at the first wall.
5 PrismOnly n differs. Red 1.773, orange 1.781, yellow 1.786, green 1.795, blue 1.812, violet 1.842. Violet bends most. In a raindrop violet is bent more, so it reaches our eyes from drops lower in the sky, inside the red.
6 Lens6 feet past the lens. At f = 4, 4 feet; at f = 9, 9 feet. f = 4 is stronger: it bends rays more. Focusing close, the eye's lens gets stronger and its f smaller.
Exit ticketAny one of: reflection (angle out = minus angle in), refraction (Snell's law), total internal reflection (reflect when n sin i is more than 1), the lens rule. Real light is a wave and needs no program; this model only follows rays, and only where it was told surfaces are.

Adjusting it

  • Middle school: Sections 1, 2 and 3, with the Program panel used only to change one number.
  • Physics: have students derive the critical angle from n before they test it, and compare 41.1° with the beam's behaviour at 40 and 45.
  • Math: the mirror bench is a right-triangle problem: work out where the dot lands with tangent before pressing Save.
  • Computer science: read how every beam keeps x and y and cuts each straight run into steps equal moves, and why that lands exactly on a surface where a fixed step would overshoot.
  • Handing work in: ☰ Menu → Save World downloads a file you can open and inspect, programs included.

Quick troubleshooting

  • Old beams are still there after a change: that is on purpose, so two settings can be compared. The red CLEAR button in the middle of the hall wipes them all.
  • A message says a value has no answer: that is Section 4, at a glass angle past 41.1°.
  • A beam flew off the board: an angle was too steep, or a minus sign is missing. Change it back and press Save.
  • Changing one prism colour does nothing: the colours wait for the white beam. Press the white beam's green ▶.
  • Lots of beams have stopped drawing: the hall has a limit on marks. Press CLEAR.
  • Things look slow: a slower computer shows the same light in slower motion. The numbers do not change.
