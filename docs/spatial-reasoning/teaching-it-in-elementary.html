How to teach spatial reasoning

The training studies that produced those effect sizes were not doing anything exotic. This is what they were actually doing, turned into four moves an elementary teacher can make on Monday — and the four mistakes that turn spatial work into a nice activity that trains nothing.

The good news first

The 217 studies in the largest meta-analysis used wildly different methods — video games, technical drawing courses, physical manipulatives, semester-long classes, single sessions — and the gains held up across nearly all of them. Spatial ability is unusually responsive to being practised at all, which means you do not need a specific programme, a purchase, or a curriculum change. You need to do some of it, on purpose, more than once.

Three findings from that same body of work shape how to spend the time. Gains persist beyond the training. They transfer to untrained spatial tasks and to mathematics. And the students who start lowest gain the most, which is the argument for doing it with everybody rather than with a club.

The four moves

Each one is a change to how you already talk and what you already ask, not an extra thing in the timetable.

  • 1 · Use spatial language deliberately, and make students use it back Above, below, between, behind, edge, face, corner, parallel, cross-section, quarter turn, mirror image, from underneath. The amount of spatial language a child hears predicts their spatial performance, and the effect is not subtle. The cheap version of this move: stop saying “that one” and “over there” while pointing. Say where it is. Then ask a student to say where it is, and hold out for the words rather than accepting a point.
  • 2 · Build and take apart real things — then draw them Construction toys, boxes, junk modelling, folded paper, taking apart a broken appliance. The step people skip is the second one: build it, then draw it from a view you cannot see — from above, from behind, in cross-section. Building alone exercises spatial relations; the drawing is what forces rotation and visualisation, which are the sub-skills that transfer.
  • 3 · Ask for a prediction before every reveal “Before I turn it — draw what you think the other side looks like.” “If I fold this, will the star be inside or outside?” A prediction that is committed to and then checked is the entire mechanism: it forces the child to build the mental image rather than wait to be shown one. Voting with hands works, a quick sketch works better, and being wrong must cost nothing at all.
  • 4 · Let them gesture, and gesture yourself Hand movement is not fidgeting during spatial work — it is a genuine external scratchpad, and studies find that children encouraged to gesture solve more spatial problems than children asked to keep still. Turn your own hand when you describe a rotation. Ask “show me with your hands” before asking for words, which also gives your students with the least spatial vocabulary a way in.

Four mistakes that waste the time

  • Making it a graded task. Spatial anxiety is measurable, behaves like maths anxiety, and suppresses performance independently of ability. A spatial activity with a mark on it recruits exactly the children who most need the practice into avoiding it. Ungraded, repeated, low-stakes is not softness — it is what the anxiety research says the training requires.
  • Letting the physical object do the work. If a child can pick the shape up and turn it every time, they never have to picture it. Handle it, then take it away and ask the question. The moment of not being able to see it is the training.
  • Assuming screen time counts. A 3D world where a student walks around, builds, rotates and predicts is spatial work. A video of one is not. The distinction is whether the child is making a spatial decision, and it is worth applying to any tool including ours.
  • Doing it once, brilliantly. One superb afternoon of spatial work produces one afternoon of gains. Ten minutes a week for a term is the shape the studies actually used, and it beats a project week every time.

Where it already lives in your curriculum

Nothing here needs a new slot. Spatial reasoning is sitting unlabelled inside lessons you already teach, and naming it is most of the work.

SubjectWhere the spatial work already is
MathematicsNets and solids, symmetry, transformations, coordinates, scale, fractions of a shape, reading any graph
SciencePhases of the Moon, the water cycle, cross-sections of anything living, plate tectonics, circuits, molecular models
GeographyMaps, contour lines, aerial views, giving and following directions, scale
Design & technologyEvery plan, every model, every “how will these two parts join”
ArtPerspective, drawing from observation, sculpture, pattern and tessellation
ComputingAnything with coordinates or rotation — turtle graphics, sprites, and 3D building or coding

The Moon phases example is the one worth trying first, because it is the clearest demonstration that a topic can be taught for years without the spatial idea ever landing. A child who can recite the phases and cannot say where the Sun is standing has learned a sequence, not a system.
