Spatial reasoning is the missing piece of STEM — and it is trainable

The evidence behind Edusim, on one page you can print and forward without editing. Written for a curriculum director, an instructional coach, or anybody who has to justify a tool before a class can use it.

📋 The IT & deployment brief

The finding

Spatial reasoning — picturing an object, turning it in your mind, imagining how parts fit together — is one of the strongest long-run predictors of who succeeds in science, technology, engineering and mathematics. It is also one of the most trainable cognitive skills that has been measured, and it is almost entirely absent from the school day.

That is a gap statement rather than a product pitch, and it is the reason this brief leads with research instead of with features.

Spatial skills are trainable

Across 217 training studies, spatial training worked — a gain researchers describe as roughly seven IQ points on spatial tasks.

Uttal et al. (2013), Psychological Bulletin
0.96 g

Double the effect in early childhood

For children aged 0–8, hands-on spatial play and 3D manipulatives produced roughly twice the effect — precisely the K–8 audience.

“Is Early Spatial Skills Training Effective?” (2020), Frontiers in Psychology — 20 studies
0.28 g

It transfers to mathematics

Across 29 controlled studies and 3,765 students, spatial training lifted mathematics scores — and 3D tools transferred better than flat 2D ones.

“Effects of Spatial Training on Mathematics Performance” (2022) — meta-analysis
+7.08 pts

3D beats 2D in a real classroom

Students learning through 3D modelling gained +7.08 points on the Purdue Spatial Visualization Test, against +1.76 for the control group.

Empirical classroom study, PSVT:R

Effect size (Hedges’s g) is how education researchers measure whether an approach genuinely moves a class, in units of a standard deviation. Anything near 0.40 is considered a solid educational win. Spatial training clears that bar for the general population and doubles it for young children.

The longitudinal argument

Project TALENT followed hundreds of thousands of American students from high school onward across several decades. It found that spatial ability in adolescence predicts later STEM achievement even after accounting for mathematical and verbal ability — that is, it carries information about a student’s future that test scores in the two subjects schools already measure do not.

Researchers have since described spatial thinking as the “missing piece” of STEM education: highly predictive, highly trainable, and largely untaught. For a curriculum leader, that combination is unusual. Most identified gaps are either not predictive enough to prioritise or not trainable enough to act on.

What Edusim claims, precisely

Edusim meets ESSA Tier IV: “Demonstrates a Rationale.” That tier describes a practice with a well-specified logic model grounded in high-quality research, together with an effort to study its effects. It is a fair and checkable description of what has been built here, and we state it rather than a vaguer claim on purpose.

What the studies tested, and what they did not. The research cited above examined 3D learning environments, spatial training and block-based coding as a category — not Edusim specifically. Edusim was built to match what that research says works, and the Research Library shows the match feature by feature: the stretch-to-shape tool, the 15° rotation snaps, the blocks-to-JavaScript bridge. No efficacy study of Edusim itself has been run. We would welcome one.

Most free classroom tools claim nothing about evidence. Many paid tools claim more than they can support. Claiming exactly what is true is both more useful to you and more defensible in a board meeting.

How the research became the product

What the research saysWhat Edusim does about it
Gains are largest in early childhoodBuilt for K–8 rather than for secondary CAD: four primitives, no menus of tools, and a first model inside one class period
3D manipulation transfers better than 2DEvery task happens in a walk-around 3D world at a fixed human scale — the camera is a five-foot person, so size is judged against something real
Mental rotation is the trainable coreRotation is a first-class action with 15° snaps, so a child can form and test a prediction about a turn rather than nudge until it looks right
Spatial anxiety suppresses performanceNothing is graded, nothing is irreversible except one clearly-marked button, and a wrecked world reloads clean from the menu
Coding plus spatial work outperforms either aloneEvery object is programmable, in drag-and-drop blocks or in real JavaScript, with one switch between the two views of the same program
Transfer improves with explicit spatial languageEvery world carries activity boards that name a nearby object, set it a task, and print the exact blocks — the vocabulary arrives with the task

Every row above is argued at length, with its sources, in the Research Library’s “In Edusim” chapter.

What it costs, and what it collects

  • Cost: nothing. There is no paid tier, no trial, no per-seat licence and no upgrade.
  • Student data collected: none. No accounts of any kind, so there is no roster, no login and no record connecting work to a child. See the privacy page.
  • Deployment: none. It is a web page. It runs on the Chromebooks a school already has, including in ephemeral mode.
  • Ready on day one: a library of curricular worlds and nine written lesson plans with printable student cards.

The trade-off, stated up front. Because there are no accounts there is no teacher dashboard, no rostering, no gradebook and no cross-device save. Students save work in the browser and hand it in as an exported file. If your adoption depends on a dashboard, Edusim is the wrong tool and we would rather you knew now than found out in week three.
