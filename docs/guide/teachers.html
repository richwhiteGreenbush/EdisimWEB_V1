🍎 For teachers

Lesson plan ideas

Nine plans, each written for a single middle-school period: two on the core skills — building a model out of primitives, and programming it with blocks — then one per world. Each is a shape rather than a script: a hook, time to explore, one thing built, and a way to end that is not "pack up". Pair each with the matching student card.

Before you start

  • No accounts, no setup. It is a web page. Open it, and you are in the park.
  • Work saves itself in the browser on that device. Re-loading a world from the menu gives a clean copy — useful between classes.
  • Save World exports a file. Students can hand one in, or take one home to carry on.
  • Everything is editable. If a class wrecks a world, load it again from the menu; nothing is permanent.
  • The Model Builder needs no files and no accounts. Four shapes under Menu → Create Model, a hammer icon on each piece, and one irreversible button (Render Model) at the end. Worth demonstrating that button once, on the board, before anybody presses it themselves.
  • Uploading a picture is optional but powerful. Apply Texture can wrap any .png or .jpg from the device around a shape. If your setting restricts what students may upload, say so up front — the colour swatches do the same job without it.
  • The blocks are Control, Motion and Looks — nine of them, no typing. A student who has met Scratch will be running in two minutes.
  • One gotcha worth teaching directly: move X/Y/Z move along the world's axes, not the object's facing. Rotating something does not change where it will travel. It is a genuinely useful idea — the difference between turning and moving — and every quick start is built around it.

🔨 The Model Builder

Design & Technology · Maths · Art

Objective. Students decompose a familiar object into simple solids, rebuild it to a stated scale, and explain the choices they made.

1 · Hook 5 min

Hold up a real object — a chair, a lamp, a water bottle. Ask the class to describe it using only the words cube, sphere, cylinder and pyramid. Write the parts list on the board. Nobody touches a computer yet.

2 · Explore 10 min

Everyone adds one of each of the four shapes and plays with the three drags — corner to stretch, middle to slide, green ball to move it anywhere including straight up. Set one task: make a cube into something a metre long and a hand thick. Empty World (Load World → Empty World) gives the class an uncluttered field to do this in.

3 · Build 20 min

Tutorial 1, the snowman, together to the end of stacking; then students build the object from the board on their own. Rule: fewest pieces that still reads as the thing.

4 · Share 5–10 min

Everyone renders. Walk the room: can you tell what each one is without being told? That is the whole assessment.

Discussion questions

  1. Which of your pieces is doing the most work — the one that makes it recognisable? Could you delete any others?
  2. You are five feet tall in this world. How did you check your model was the right size, and how sure are you?
  3. Render Model can't be undone. When should you press it, and what would you check first?

Skills used

  • Decomposition into primitives
  • Scale and proportion against a known reference
  • Sequencing an irreversible step last

Extension

Give pairs the same five-piece budget and the same object. Compare results — identical constraints, different solutions, which is the point.

🧩 The Coding Blocks

Computing · Maths

Objective. Students write and debug a looping sequence, predict the effect of changing one parameter, and explain why turning an object does not change where it travels.

1 · Hook 5 min

Program one object on the board with forever → rotate 15 degrees. Then ask the class to predict what rotate 90 will look like before you run it. Take a vote.

2 · Explore 10 min

Everyone programs anything they like — a bench, a dinosaur, a model of their own — using only Control and Motion. Nothing to hand in; the point is to find the palette.

3 · Build 20 min

The patrol problem: make something go out and come back, facing the right way. Let them discover that a square route is impossible with these blocks, then unpack why.

4 · Share 5–10 min

Two or three demonstrations, then the duplicate block as a finale — one model becomes a row of them in front of the class.

Discussion questions

  1. Why can't you drive something round a square with these blocks? What single new block would fix it?
  2. +10% twenty times is not +200%. Work out what it actually is, and explain why to someone who guessed 200.
  3. A copy inherits everything except the duplicate block. What would happen if it didn't?

Coding used

  • forever · repeat · wait
  • move X/Y/Z · rotate
  • change size · change colour · duplicate

Extension

Give the class a target — "make it look like it is breathing" — and no blocks. Let them work out the sequence themselves, then compare approaches.

🌳 The Park

Science · Geography · Design

Objective. Students explain how a designed landscape shapes the way people use it, and use a repeat loop to animate part of a food web.

1 · Hook 5 min

Walk everyone to the pond and ask: why is there a path here and not there? Why is the bandstand at the end of the long axis and not in the middle?

2 · Explore 10–15 min

Ten minutes free-roaming, reading placards. Ask each student to find one thing that was designed rather than natural, and one living thing that depends on another.

3 · Build 15–20 min

Quick Start 1 — programming the geese. Then the pond food-web discussion: sun → algae → insects → fish → heron.

4 · Share 5–10 min

Pairs demonstrate their geese. Whose looks most like a real bird, and what number did they change to get there?

Discussion questions

  1. Olmsted put walkers, riders and carriages on separate levels. What modern thing works the same way?
  2. The meadow is cut once a year instead of weekly. Who wins and who loses?
  3. Your geese move in a straight line. What would you need to make them turn a corner?

Coding used

  • Send the geese out for a paddle
  • Grow the maple from a sapling

Extension

Have students redesign a corner of the park by adding their own objects, then justify each choice in three sentences.

🖼️ The Museum

Art · Computing

Objective. Students identify features of six art movements and use a forever loop to turn a static sculpture into a kinetic one.

1 · Hook 5 min

Stand in front of the Pointillist painting and ask what it is made of. Then get everyone to walk right up to it, and back again.

2 · Explore 10–15 min

Each student picks one painting and reads its plaque. In pairs, they describe their movement to each other without naming it and see if the partner can find it.

3 · Build 15–20 min

Quick Start 1 — the mobile. Then challenge 3: two sculptures turning at different rates, viewed together.

4 · Share 5–10 min

Gallery walk. Each student shows their kinetic piece and gives it a title and a one-line wall label.

Discussion questions

  1. Calder invented the mobile in the 1930s. What made a moving sculpture such a radical idea?
  2. Does a slower rotation make a sculpture feel more or less serious? Why might that be?
  3. Your rotate block turns the object about its own centre. What would change if it turned about a point outside itself?

Coding used

  • Set the mobile turning
  • Make the glass study change colour

Extension

Students use Draw to paint a work in the style of one movement, inflate it, and hang it in the gallery with a plaque of their own.

📚 The Library

Information literacy · Computing · History

Objective. Students navigate a classification system by number, and use a loop with a sign change to move an object out and back.

1 · Hook 5 min

Set a scavenger hunt: find the aisle where a book about volcanoes would live, using only the hanging signs.

2 · Explore 10–15 min

Students explore the card catalog and work out how you would find a book by author before computers — and how many drawers that took.

3 · Build 15–20 min

Quick Start 1 — the book cart. The sign flip in the second loop is the teaching moment: turning is not moving.

4 · Share 5–10 min

Race the carts. Whose does a full round trip fastest, and what did they change?

Discussion questions

  1. Dewey split all human knowledge into ten numbers in 1876. What is missing, and what would you add?
  2. Why does the cart need −1 in the second loop when it has already turned around?
  3. Libraries lend about two billion items a year in the US alone, and nearly all come back. Why do you think that is?

Coding used

  • Send the book cart back to the stacks
  • Give the globe a spin

Extension

Students design and label their own classification for something they know well — music, games, sport — and defend the top ten categories.

🌙 The Moon

Space science · Physics · History

Objective. Students explain why the lunar sky is black and the flag cannot wave, and program a vehicle to follow a bounded route.

1 · Hook 5 min

Ask before anyone reads a sign: it is the middle of the lunar day. Why is the sky black?

2 · Explore 10–15 min

Find and read the placards on the flag, the footprints and the ALSEP mirror. The laser reflector is still in use today — that usually surprises people.

3 · Build 15–20 min

Quick Start 1 — the buggy survey, with the real-world constraint that crews never drove further than they could walk back.

4 · Share 5–10 min

Students state their buggy's range in feet and explain why a longer loop would have been a bad idea in 1971.

Discussion questions

  1. No air means no wind. So how is the flag held out flat?
  2. A lunar day is 29½ Earth days — two weeks of sunlight, then two weeks of night. What does that mean for a solar-powered base?
  3. The footprints could last a million years. What does that tell you about weather on the Moon?

Coding used

  • Take the buggy out for rock samples
  • Set the Earth turning

Extension

Design a base for the two-week lunar night. What has to be stored, and how much? Students add their own structures to the site.

🔴 On Mars

Space science · Biology · Engineering

Objective. Students identify what a human outpost must supply that Earth provides free, and program a vehicle and an aircraft on different axes.

1 · Hook 5 min

Walk into the dome and shut up about it for a moment. Then ask: what is this actually for? (It is not warmth — it is holding an atmosphere in.)

2 · Explore 10–15 min

Tour the grow bay, life support and the ice drill. Each student lists three things the base makes that we get for nothing at home.

3 · Build 15–20 min

Quick Starts 1 and 2 — the rover on Z, the helicopter on Y. Good moment to make the world-axis rule explicit.

4 · Share 5–10 min

Compare rover routes. Introduce the 22-light-minute delay and ask what that does to driving something by remote control.

Discussion questions

  1. Martian air is 95% carbon dioxide. NASA has already made oxygen from it on Mars — how?
  2. Why is move Y the hardest one to justify on Mars, when the air is 1% as thick as ours?
  3. A sol is 24 hours 37 minutes. Why does that slowly become a problem for a crew?

Coding used

  • Send the rover out on survey
  • Fly the scout copter

Extension

Students budget a crew of six for one Martian year (687 days) — water, oxygen, food — and add the storage they would need to the base.

🦖 Dinosaur Island

Biology · Earth science · Computing

Objective. Students explain how fossils form and what a trackway reveals, then animate an animal's behaviour rather than just its position.

1 · Hook 5 min

Start at the dig, not the dinosaurs. Ask how anyone knows any of this before you let them go and look at a T. rex.

2 · Explore 10–15 min

The string grid, the plaster jackets and the trackway placards. Each student writes one sentence on what a trackway tells you that a skeleton cannot.

3 · Build 15–20 min

Quick Start 1 — the grazing Triceratops. Emphasise the wait: the pause is what turns walking into browsing.

4 · Share 5–10 min

Half the class runs a herd (challenge 1), half runs a lone predator (challenge 2). Watch both and discuss what makes each read as an animal.

Discussion questions

  1. Almost nothing that dies becomes a fossil. What has to happen, and how fast?
  2. Some Edmontosaurus fossils have T. rex bite marks that healed. What does the healing prove?
  3. Birds are dinosaurs, not just descended from them. What evidence in this world supports that?

Coding used

  • Set the Triceratops grazing
  • Put the Quetzalcoatlus on patrol

Extension

Students research one animal here and rewrite its placard from a first-person point of view, then place it in the world.

🫀 Fantastic Voyage

Biology · Health · Maths

Objective. Students locate the major organs and describe what connects them, then use loops and timing to model a real biological rate.

1 · Hook 5 min

Walk in along the artery and stop under the rib cage. Ask what has to be true about ribs for you to be able to breathe.

2 · Explore 10–15 min

The chart gallery at the far end. Each pair takes one body system and traces it from start to finish on the diagram.

3 · Build 15–20 min

Quick Start 1 — the beating heart. Then challenge 1: calculate the wait for exactly 70 beats a minute.

4 · Share 5–10 min

Run every heart in the room at once at different rates and ask which looks like rest and which looks like a sprint.

Discussion questions

  1. Everything here is 15–20 times life size. If the model liver is 8 feet across, how big is the real one?
  2. +6% then −6% does not return you to where you started. Why not — and what does that tell you about percentages?
  3. The right lung has three lobes and the left only two. What is using the missing space?

Coding used

  • Make the heart beat
  • Launch the micro-sub

Extension

Students take a resting pulse, then after 30 seconds of stepping, and program two hearts side by side to match both measurements.
