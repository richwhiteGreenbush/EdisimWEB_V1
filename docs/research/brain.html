🧠 The Brain

What a 3D world does that a flat page can't

A textbook diagram asks a child to do two jobs at once: rebuild the 3D object in their head, and then think about it. A live 3D world does the rebuilding for them — and neuroimaging studies show what that frees the brain to do.

Working memory

Cognitive offloading: the GPU does the imagining

Flat diagrams suffer from ambiguity, occlusion, and missing depth cues, so before a student can solve a spatial problem on paper, they must first spend working memory reconstructing the 3D object the flat picture implies. Neuroimaging studies using fMRI and EEG find that people solving 3D geometry puzzles in interactive 3D settings show higher neural efficiency in the prefrontal cortex than people doing equivalent tasks on flat 2D screens: the environment's rendering engine carries the sensory load, so the brain's limited working memory goes to actual problem-solving.

The effect is biggest for students with lower starting spatial ability. On paper, they're often overwhelmed by the mental reconstruction step before the real thinking begins; in a live 3D scene, the model is simply there — walkable, turnable, checkable — acting as an external scaffold that lets them work at the level of their reasoning instead of the level of their imagination.

In Edusim: nothing is a diagram. The T. rex, the cut-away volcano, the human heart — a student walks around each one, and the question "what does it look like from the other side?" is answered by their feet, not by straining their working memory.

Two spatial systems

Egocentric, allocentric — and the translator between them

Cognitive neuroscience describes two distinct spatial systems in the brain, and a third region that translates between them. A 3D world a child can both walk through and program is one of the few learning tools that exercises all three at once.

👁️ Egocentric — "where is it, from me?"

Space anchored to your own body: the block is to my left; the door is ahead. Handled by the brain's dorsal "where" pathway and parietal cortex, and it's the system children develop first.

In Edusim: walking the world with the arrow keys, placing a shape "in front of me," steering a robot with move forward.

🗺️ Allocentric — "where is it, on the map?"

Space independent of you: a bird's-eye layout, object-to-object relations, global X-Y-Z coordinates. Handled by the ventral "what" pathway and the hippocampus — the brain's cognitive map-maker.

In Edusim: learning a world's layout, coding movement along world axes, planning where the next building goes.

🔄 The translator — retrosplenial cortex

The RSC converts between the two frames — between "on my left" and "on the north side." An fMRI study of 3D sketching in virtual environments found this kind of design work actively engages it. Fluid switching between local view and global map is a hallmark of expert spatial thinkers in architecture, geology, and engineering.

In Edusim: writing code that moves an object along a world axis while standing next to it — one action, both frames, every time.

Confidence

Low stakes, lower spatial anxiety

Spatial anxiety is real and measurable: a domain-specific stress that eats the very working memory a spatial task needs, depressing performance and teaching kids to avoid the whole category. It feeds a loop — avoid spatial play, fall further behind, feel more anxious.

Sandboxes break the loop because mistakes cost nothing. A block placed wrong is deleted and rebuilt; no materials are wasted and nobody is watching. Researchers point to exactly this low-risk, high-feedback quality to explain why digital 3D practice reduces spatial anxiety while building technical confidence.

The equity finding

On traditional paper tests like the Mental Rotations Test, boys have historically outscored girls — a gap widely linked to the underrepresentation of women in engineering, physics, and computer science, and entangled with spatial anxiety rather than any fixed difference in ability. The intervention studies carry the hopeful result: 3D, game-like spatial training lifts girls' scores the most, shrinking the traditional gap — and in some studies erasing or reversing it. Give every child the same rich spatial playground, and the difference the paper tests measured starts to disappear.

In Edusim: no timers, no scores, no leaderboard, no chat, no audience. Deleting a piece and trying again is a one-click, zero-shame act — which is precisely the practice environment the anxiety research prescribes.
