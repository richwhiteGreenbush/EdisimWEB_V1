📊 The Evidence

What the studies measured

Not one hopeful study — meta-analyses that pool hundreds of them. A meta-analysis gathers every well-run experiment on a question and measures the combined result, which makes it the strongest kind of evidence education research produces. On spatial training, the combined results are unusually clear.

Finding 1

Spatial ability is a skill, not a gift

For decades, many people assumed you were either "good at spatial stuff" or you weren't. The largest meta-analysis on the question — Uttal and colleagues (2013), pooling 217 training studies in Psychological Bulletin — settled it: spatial skills respond strongly to practice at every age, with an average effect size of g = 0.47. Researchers describe a gain that size as roughly equivalent to seven IQ points on spatial tasks.

Two details of that meta-analysis matter most for schools:

  • The gains last. Improvements persisted for months after training ended.
  • The gains transfer. Students got better at spatial tasks they were never trained on — practice with one kind of building or rotating improved spatial thinking in general.

Finding 2

The younger the learner, the bigger the gain

A 2020 meta-analysis in Frontiers in Psychology asked whether spatial training works for the youngest learners. Across 20 studies of children aged 0–8, the average effect was g = 0.96 — about double the general figure. Hands-on exploration, visual prompts, and 3D manipulatives in the early years can meaningfully change a child's trajectory before formal STEM schooling even begins.

This is why Edusim aims young: simple shapes, drag-and-drop code, and worlds a child can walk into land squarely in the window where the research says spatial practice pays off most.

Finding 3

Spatial practice shows up in math class

The strongest reason schools should care: a 2022 meta-analysis of 29 controlled pre/post studies with 3,765 participants found spatial training significantly improved mathematics performance (g = 0.28) while lifting spatial thinking itself (g = 0.49). Number lines, fractions, measurement, geometry, and graphs all lean on spatial representations — train the space, and the math moves.

The same analysis found something specific to how Edusim works: training with concrete materials — physical manipulatives or their high-fidelity 3D virtual equivalents — transferred to math significantly better than flat 2D exercises. A student mapping a build across X, Y, and Z is practicing coordinate geometry whether or not anyone calls it that.

Finding 4

3D beats 2D on standard tests

Researchers measure spatial growth with validated instruments — most notably the Mental Rotations Test (MRT) and the Purdue Spatial Visualization Test: Rotations (PSVT:R). In one classroom comparison, students taught through 3D computer animation and virtual modeling gained +7.08 points on the PSVT:R while the control group gained +1.76 — a contrast that isolates active 3D interaction, rather than test familiarity, as the driver.
