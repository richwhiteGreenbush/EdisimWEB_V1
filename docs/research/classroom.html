🧩 In Edusim

The research, built into the tool

Edusim isn't research-flavored — it's research-shaped. Each row below pairs a finding from the literature with the specific Edusim feature that puts it in a child's hands.

Finding → feature

Eight findings, eight design choices

The research says

Kids learn deepest by building shareable things

Seymour Papert's constructionism — the theory behind LEGO robotics and Scratch — holds that learning peaks when children construct a meaningful artifact they can show someone. Studies of construction-plus-coding tools report gains in spatial, logical, and numerical reasoning together.

Papert, Mindstorms (1980); robotics & visual-programming studies — Sources §4
So in Edusim

Create Model, then hand it to someone

Students build from stretchable primitives, render the pieces into one model, and export the whole world as a small file a classmate or teacher can open and walk through. The artifact is the assignment.

The research says

3D manipulatives beat flat 2D training

The 2022 math-transfer meta-analysis found concrete materials — physical manipulatives or high-fidelity 3D virtual equivalents — transfer to mathematics significantly better than flat 2D computer training.

“Effects of Spatial Training on Mathematics Performance” (2022) — Sources §1
So in Edusim

Stretch-to-Shape, with an anchored corner

Every primitive sits in a bounding box with grabbable corners: drag one and the opposite corner stays planted, so a cube becomes a plank, a post, a wall — while the piece stays honestly on the ground plane. That's intrinsic spatial transformation, made tactile.

The research says

Angular reasoning needs practice with real angles

Mental rotation — the most-studied spatial skill — improves through repeated, feedback-rich rotation of objects, and connecting rotation to number is where geometry begins.

Uttal et al. (2013); MRT/PSVT:R literature — Sources §1–2
So in Edusim

Rotation rings that snap to 15°

Turning a piece snaps in 15° steps, so square corners, 45° braces and 30° roof pitches are hittable on a trackpad — and every turn quietly says its number. A child aligning a brace at 45° is doing applied trigonometry.

The research says

Block coding frees working memory for the actual thinking

Text syntax overwhelms novices; block-based visual languages cut that load so cognition goes to the algorithm and the spatial transformations. Studies show tangible and block-based coding builds computational thinking and spatial reasoning simultaneously.

Visual-programming & computational-thinking studies — Sources §4
So in Edusim

Blocks first — and the same program as JavaScript

Every object is programmable with snap-together blocks: repeat 4 times → move forward 8 → rotate 90 drives a square. One toggle shows the identical program as real JavaScript, so the bridge to text code is built into the panel, not left for later.

The research says

Code plus space is its own powerful skill

Researchers call it spatial computational thinking: decomposing a structure into parts, then writing loops and conditions that build or steer it. Debugging the program means debugging your spatial logic — an externalized mental model you can fix.

Spatial computational thinking literature — Sources §4
So in Edusim

Programs that move things through the world

Blocks steer objects along their own facing, loops close squares (360 ÷ sides), the marker pen draws the path so the program's geometry is literally visible on the ground, and every ready-made world ships challenges that ask for exactly these programs.

The research says

Both spatial systems should get exercise

Walking a space trains body-centered (egocentric) processing; maps and coordinates train world-centered (allocentric) mapping; translating between them engages the retrosplenial cortex — the flexibility expert spatial thinkers show.

Neuroimaging & navigation literature — Sources §3
So in Edusim

Worlds you walk, and code that uses axes

Every world is explored first-person at a child's own eye height — and the same child then writes code that moves objects along world axes, plans layouts, and reasons about where things are from nowhere in particular. One tool, both frames, constantly.

The research says

Context makes spatial learning stick

Large explorable environments exercise cognitive mapping, and situating spatial tasks inside meaningful places — a dig site, a city, a landmark — anchors the geometry in narrative, geology, history and biology.

Virtual-environment navigation studies — Sources §3, §5
So in Edusim

A library of ready worlds whose signage teaches

Dinosaur Island, a cut-away volcano, the human body, the Moon, Ancient Egypt, the Colosseum — more than thirty walk-scale places dotted with placards and activity boards, plus live in-world browser panels so research happens inside the workspace.

The research says

Low-stakes practice closes gaps

Spatial anxiety suppresses performance and drives avoidance — and it falls in safe, consequence-free environments. 3D practice lifts girls' mental-rotation scores the most, shrinking a gap tied to STEM underrepresentation. Access matters too: training only helps the kids who can reach it.

Spatial-anxiety & gender-equity studies — Sources §2–3
So in Edusim

Free, no accounts, no chat, runs on Chromebooks

It's a web page: nothing to install, nobody to sign up, no strangers, no timers or scores. Work saves on the student's own device. Delete a piece and try again — the cost of a mistake is zero, for every student, on the hardware schools already own.
