🔬 Research-based practices

A research-based learning tool for kids

Edusim is built on one of the best-supported findings in learning science: spatial reasoning — the ability to picture, turn, and build things in your mind — is trainable, it predicts success in science and math for decades, and it grows fastest when kids build and code in hands-on 3D environments. This library walks through that research in plain language, shows how each finding is built into Edusim, and links every claim to its source.

The headline numbers

What the studies found

Effect size (Hedges's g) is how researchers measure whether a teaching approach genuinely works — roughly, how far it moves the whole class, in units of a standard deviation. Anything around 0.4 is considered a solid educational win. Spatial training clears that bar, and for young children it doubles it.

0.47 g

Spatial skills are trainable

Across 217 studies, spatial training worked — and researchers describe the average gain as roughly the size of a seven-IQ-point improvement on spatial tasks.

Uttal et al. (2013), Psychological Bulletin — meta-analysis of 217 studies
0.96 g

Young kids gain the most

For children aged 0–8, hands-on spatial play and 3D manipulatives produced roughly double the effect — the years Edusim's audience is in.

“Is Early Spatial Skills Training Effective?” (2020), Frontiers in Psychology — 20 studies
0.28 g

It transfers to math

Across 29 controlled studies (3,765 students), spatial training lifted mathematics scores too — and 3D tools transferred better than flat 2D ones.

“Effects of Spatial Training on Mathematics Performance” (2022) — meta-analysis
+7.08 pts

3D beats 2D in class

Students learning through 3D modeling gained +7.08 points on a standard spatial test (the PSVT:R), against +1.76 for the control group.

Empirical classroom study, Purdue Spatial Visualization Test — see The Evidence
Why it matters

Spatial reasoning is the skill schools forgot

Spatial reasoning is the mental toolkit behind reading a map, assembling a model, imagining a molecule, rotating a shape in your head, or picturing what a building looks like from the other side. Long-term studies that followed students for decades — most famously Project TALENT, which tracked hundreds of thousands of Americans from high school onward — found that spatial ability in adolescence is one of the strongest predictors of who later succeeds in science, technology, engineering, and math, even after accounting for math and verbal scores.

Yet most school days train words and arithmetic, and almost never train space. Researchers have called spatial thinking the “missing piece” of STEM education: hugely predictive, highly trainable, and largely absent from the curriculum.

That gap is exactly what a 3D sandbox is for. When a child stretches a cube into a wall, rotates a roof brace to 45°, walks around their own model, or writes a loop that places blocks along an axis, they are doing spatial training — the kind the meta-analyses measured — while thinking they are just building something cool.

Honesty note: the studies in this library tested 3D learning environments, spatial training, and block-based coding in general — the category of tool Edusim belongs to — rather than Edusim specifically. We built Edusim to match what that research says works, and this library shows the match, claim by claim.
